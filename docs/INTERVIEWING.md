# How Wonkite interviews

Wonkite's questions follow rules borrowed from people who interview for a living: broadcast and print journalists, oral historians, ethnographers, qualitative researchers, and the police psychologists who studied how to help witnesses remember. This page lists those rules and where each one shows up in the code.

## The sources, briefly

**John Sawatsky (journalism).** Sawatsky, a Canadian journalist, is known for teaching newsrooms how to interview. His rule is that questions should be short, open and neutral. Open questions begin with what, how, or why. Closed questions (yes or no) let the speaker end a line of inquiry with one word. Statements and opinions from the interviewer give the speaker something to react to instead of something to answer. Value words in a question colour the answer, so "What's it like being a duchess?" beats "Is it hard being a duchess?" When the speaker uses a loaded word, hand it back: "What do you mean, excessive?" His summary is that colourless questions produce colourful answers.

**James Spradley (ethnography).** Spradley's descriptive questions start wide and narrow down. A grand-tour question asks someone to walk you through a place, a time, or an activity. A mini-tour question zooms into one part of it. An example question asks for one specific instance. An experience question asks for something that happened. A native-language question asks what they call a thing, so their words don't get replaced by yours.

**Irving Seidman (qualitative research).** Seidman's in-depth interviewing runs in three stages: how the person came to the subject, the concrete details of the experience, and only then what it means to them. He advises asking "how" rather than "why," since "why" invites justification, and asking people to reconstruct an experience rather than simply remember it. Listen more, talk less, let silences run, and don't reinforce answers in ways that steer them.

**Oral history practice.** Oral history guides recommend brief, open questions from an outline rather than a script. When the speaker touches on something interesting, ask for an example or a description. Let the conversation wander when they raise something new. If they skip something you want, note it and come back to it later. Don't talk for more than about a fifth of the interview, and don't fill pauses.

**Studs Terkel (radio and oral history).** Terkel brought no written questions, improvised from what people said, and kept himself out of the conversation. His gift was curiosity and letting people talk.

**The cognitive interview (Fisher and Geiselman).** Developed to help witnesses recall more accurately, it uses four techniques. Report everything, since small details cue bigger ones. Mentally reinstate the context: the surroundings and how you felt. Recall in a different order, such as working backward from the end. Describe the event from a different point of view.

**Ira Glass (radio storytelling).** A story has two building blocks: the anecdote, which is the sequence of what happened, and the moment of reflection on what it means. The anecdote comes first.

**Motivational interviewing (OARS).** Counsellors use open questions, affirmations, reflections and summaries. Wonkite uses only the first. Affirming and reflecting are things a human listener does well and a small program does badly, and Seidman and Sawatsky both warn that reinforcing an answer steers the next one.

## The rules Wonkite follows

**1. One short, open, neutral question at a time.** Every follow-up begins with what, how, where, who, or "tell me." None can be answered yes or no, and none carries a describing word of Wonkite's own. Double-barrelled questions ("…and why?") were removed from the question bank, because a second question gives the speaker a way to answer the easier half.

**2. Use their words.** When an answer contains a describing word such as weird, fine, peaceful or brutal, Wonkite hands it back: "Weird how?" "What do you mean, fine?" "What made it peaceful?" Otherwise it builds the question around a phrase from the answer ("your friend Grant," "that long hot day," "the little place by the station") rather than paraphrasing.

**3. Story first, meaning last.** Each line of inquiry, or thread, moves through three stages:

| Stage | Question types | Drawn from |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Grand tour: "Tell me about that long hot day." | Spradley, Seidman |
| Middle | Detail, context ("Put yourself back there. What's around you?"), example, people, what happened next or just before, another point of view | Spradley, cognitive interview, oral history |
| Close | Reflection: "Looking back on all that, what do you make of it?" | Seidman, Glass |

Reflection is only asked after four questions on a thread, so the speaker has told the story before being asked what it meant. After the reflection, Wonkite moves to a new thread.

**4. Follow what they offer.** When an answer mentions a feeling, Wonkite asks about that moment ("What was going on in your head right then?"). When it says "then" or "after," Wonkite asks what happened next or just before. A very short answer gets "Tell me more" or "Go on," once.

**5. Come back to what was skipped.** When an answer offers more than one thread, Wonkite follows one and notes the others. Moving between threads, it sometimes returns to one of them: "Earlier you mentioned the pier. Tell me more about that."

**6. Say almost nothing.** Wonkite never comments, praises, summarises or agrees. Its share of the words on the page stays small, and it waits for as long as you take.

**7. Avoid "why."** "Why" mostly survives in the question bank's openers. Follow-ups ask how and what instead.

The online question service in `worker/` is given the same rules in its instructions, along with a hint naming which type of question the offline engine would have asked, so both modes ask the same kind of question at the same point in a thread.
